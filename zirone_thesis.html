Between Data and Place:
Reading the Territory

Master's Thesis Abstract — 2026

Abstract

Living in a territory means continuously constructing a representation of it: through daily experience, memory, relationships, and, increasingly, data. Yet between the information available and people's ability to interpret and use it, there exists a profound gap — fuelled by distrust, poor digital literacy, and limited civic participation. This gap is not neutral: it produces data deserts1, where lack of awareness generates inequalities and less effective services.

The project aims to explore how citizens integrate quantitative data with local knowledge, mental maps, and lived territorial experience to imagine scenarios of adaptation and transformation.

The provisional research question guiding this design idea unfolds across interconnected levels:

How can a narrative information interface, designed from local territorial data, be structured so that it does not simply translate data into images, but puts them in dialogue with the memory, experience, and local knowledge of the community?

To what extent does emotional and relational anchoring to a lived space act as a lever capable of reducing cognitive distance from data and motivating active community participation?

And finally: how can a co-design process transform visualisation from an analytical output into a space for negotiating collective meaning, reinforcing cohesion and belonging?

Starting from a specific territorial context and a limited sample, the project adopts a mixed approach: qualitative field research, co-design sessions with the local community, and analysis of the socio-cultural dynamics that structure the reading of the territory. The territory is therefore understood not as mere physical space, but as an interweaving of relationships, practices, memories, and values2 — dimensions that affect belonging, identity, and conflict.

In a landscape dominated by data-driven decision making, a deliberate choice is made for a data-informed3 approach, in which data dialogues with qualitative research and the designer's critical judgement. Visualisation is not an aesthetic or analytical output, but a form of data fabric4: an informational fabric redesigned in its meaning, which makes data understandable and usable by those who inhabit that territory. Working at a local scale lowers cognitive defences, increases motivation to participate, and allows the sense of place5 to act as an emotional lever, generating engagement that abstract or national data can rarely produce.

The ultimate goal is not to build a visualisation, but a narrative interface6 through which the community can interrogate its own space, compare perspectives, and decide together. Because people do not simply "read" a chart: they negotiate collective meaning7.
